# Working a ticket

The ticket page is where a request is actually handled. Everything about it is on one screen: the conversation in the middle, its properties down the right, and the moves you can make along the top.

## Replying, and writing to yourself

There are two ways to write on a ticket and the difference matters.

**A reply** goes to the requester. It is e-mailed to them and shows up in their portal.

**An internal note** stays on the desk. The requester never sees it, in the portal or by e-mail. It is where you put "I think this is the same fault as SUP-901" and "waiting on the supplier, chased Tuesday".

They look different on screen so you can tell at a glance which is which, but the moment to check is before you send, not after. There is no unsend.

Both accept formatting and pasted screenshots, and both take attachments.

## Moving it along

The buttons along the top are the moves this ticket can make *right now*. They come from the workflow your desk configured, which is why the buttons change as the ticket progresses and why you cannot always go straight to closed.

Some transitions insist on a comment before they will run. That is deliberate: a desk that requires a sentence before resolving is a desk whose closed tickets say what was done.

## Who has it

**Assignee** is the one person responsible. Search for them; the list only offers people who may actually hold this ticket.

That last part is a rule, not a shortened list: **a ticket that belongs to a team can only be assigned to somebody on that team.** A ticket with no team at all can go to any agent — plenty of work arrives without one.

**Claim** is the same thing pointed at yourself, and it obeys the same rule.

Moving a ticket to another team releases whoever was holding it, if they are not on the new team. That is not a mistake — the ticket has gone somewhere else, and the assignment no longer makes sense. It is recorded like any other unassignment, so the trail shows it.

## Watchers

Watchers get notified when the ticket moves. The requester is one automatically, and so are you once you touch it.

Add anybody with an account — a line manager, somebody in another team who asked to be kept informed. Adding somebody as a watcher is not the same as assigning them; it does not make the ticket theirs.

## Linking tickets together

Link a ticket to another when they are related: one **duplicates** another, one **blocks** another, one **causes** another, or they simply **relate**. The link shows on both tickets, so it does not matter which end you make it from.

Duplicates are worth linking rather than just closing. The link is what tells the next person why.

## Cloning

**Clone** files the same request again. Use it for work that recurs, or when one ticket turns out to be two jobs.

What comes across is what describes the request: the subject, the description, the requester, the priority, the queue and team, the labels, and the answers to the form's own questions. What does not come across is everything that records *this* ticket being handled — the conversation, the attachments, the approvals, the clocks, and who was holding it.

You can file the copy as a different kind of request. If you do, it moves to that request type's queue, team and workflow, and any answers the new form does not ask for are dropped rather than carried along invisibly.

The two tickets are linked automatically, so the original is one click away.

## The panels down the right

- **Service levels** — the clocks on this ticket. See *Service levels*.
- **Approvals** — ask somebody to approve, and see what they said. See *Approvals*.
- **Knowledge base** — articles that look relevant, and the ones somebody attached. See *Knowledge base*.
- **Assets** — the equipment this ticket is about. See *Assets*.
